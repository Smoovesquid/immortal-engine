# The Ashenmoor

A desolate moorland east of the settled lowlands, where the peat runs black and the fog never fully lifts. The Ashenmoor was once a seat of monastic learning — the Order of the Ember Quill kept its great library here, in an abbey built from local ashstone. That was before the Silence fell. Now the abbey stands roofless and dark, the monks who remain speak no word aloud, and the moor folk dig peat to survive and fear the fog that swallows travelers whole.

## The Ruined Abbey of Saint Aldwyn

The abbey crowns a low hill at the moor's northern edge. Its walls still stand but the roof collapsed decades ago, leaving the nave open to rain and wind. The cloister garden has gone wild with black briar and pale lichen. Beneath the refectory, a locked undercroft descends into darkness — the monks permit no one entry. Carved above the door is a single word in Old Script that no living scholar has translated.

The remaining monks — perhaps a dozen — move through the ruins in grey robes, communicating only through hand signs and the ringing of small bronze bells. They tend a perpetual fire in the chapter house, feeding it with peat they cut themselves. The fire has not gone out in living memory. Travelers may shelter in the gatehouse but are forbidden from entering the inner cloister after dark.

## Greyfen Village

A peat-harvesting settlement of forty souls, built along a raised causeway that crosses the southern moor. The houses are low, turf-roofed, and damp. Smoke hangs in the lanes from the drying kilns where cut peat is cured for fuel and trade. Greyfen is the only reliable source of peat for three days' travel in any direction, which gives the village a bitter prosperity — enough to survive, never enough to leave.

The villagers distrust the monks and resent the abbey's ancient claim to the northern peat bogs. A generation ago the abbey cut peat freely; now the monks cut in silence and the village council pretends not to notice, but the resentment festers. The old causeway once connected to a trade road south, but the road has sunk into the moor. Only the peat-cutters know the safe paths now.

## The Standing Stones of Cael Druith

A circle of nine weathered stones on a windswept rise in the moor's interior. Each stone is taller than a man and carved with spirals that seem to shift in torchlight. The ground inside the circle is bare earth — nothing grows there, and rain pools in unnatural patterns around the bases.

Local legend holds that the stones predate the abbey by a thousand years and that the monks built where they did to watch over them. On nights when the fog is thickest, lights have been seen moving between the stones — cold, blue-white, and utterly silent. The peat-cutters avoid the circle. The monks visit it at the solstices, walking in slow procession from the abbey and standing among the stones for an hour before returning. No one knows what they do there.

## The Fog Pass of Harrowgate

A narrow defile cutting through a ridge of broken ashstone at the moor's eastern edge, connecting the Ashenmoor to the lands beyond. The pass is choked with fog nearly year-round — a thick, wet grey that reduces visibility to arm's length. Travelers enter and sometimes do not emerge. Those who do speak of wrong turns, of hearing voices just ahead, of walking for hours only to find themselves back at the western mouth.

The monks once maintained waymarkers through the pass — iron posts with bronze bells that rang in the wind. Most of the posts have rusted away or been pulled down. Two remain, their bells green with verdigris, and on still days their faint ringing is the only sound in the defile. Something lives in the fog, or the fog itself is alive. The villagers have a saying: "The pass takes one in ten." They are not speaking metaphorically.

## Factions

### The Order of the Ember Quill

The surviving monks of Saint Aldwyn's Abbey. Once scholars and copyists, they took a collective vow of silence after an event they call only "the Silence" — an incident in the undercroft some thirty years ago that killed half their number and left the rest mute by choice. They guard something beneath the abbey. Their leader, the Warden, communicates through written notes slipped under doors. They are not hostile but they are unyielding: the undercroft is sealed, the fire must not go out, and the standing stones must be watched. They trade nothing and ask for nothing except to be left alone.

### The Greyfen Peat-Cutters

The working folk of Greyfen village and the scattered moorland steadings. They harvest peat, cure it, and haul it south along deteriorating tracks. Their council is led by the Reeve, an elected position that carries more burden than honor. The cutters resent the monks' claim to the northern bogs and suspect the Order is hoarding something valuable beneath the abbey. Some younger cutters have begun cutting peat dangerously close to the standing stones, testing boundaries. The faction is pragmatic, suspicious of outsiders, and desperate for any trade route that does not pass through Harrowgate.

## NPCs

### Brother Maren (Order of the Ember Quill)

The abbey's herbalist and the only monk who will interact with outsiders at length. He communicates through a wax tablet and stylus, writing in a cramped, urgent hand. Maren is torn between his vow and a growing conviction that the Silence was a mistake — that what the Order guards should be understood, not merely sealed away. He will trade healing salves for news of the outside world and may, if trusted, share fragments of what he knows about the undercroft.

### Warden Thessa (Order of the Ember Quill)

The leader of the Order, a gaunt woman of sixty who has not spoken in three decades. She communicates only through written notes, and even these are terse. Thessa believes absolutely in the necessity of the Silence and views any inquiry into the undercroft as existential threat. She watches the standing stones with an intensity that borders on obsession and has been seen walking the circle alone at midnight, something the other monks find unsettling.

### Reeve Callum (Greyfen Peat-Cutters)

The elected head of Greyfen's village council. A heavyset man in his forties with peat-stained hands and a pragmatic mind. Callum wants two things: a safe trade route that bypasses Harrowgate, and access to the northern peat bogs the monks claim. He distrusts the Order but fears what might happen if the abbey's fire goes out — an old superstition he half-believes. He will hire capable travelers for scouting work and pays fairly in dried peat and salted fish.

### Fen Dysart (Greyfen Peat-Cutters)

A young peat-cutter with ambitions beyond the moor. Dysart has been cutting close to the standing stones and claims to have found carved tablets buried in the peat — tablets covered in the same spirals as the stones. She has hidden them and told no one except a traveling merchant who never returned. Dysart is brave, reckless, and convinced the tablets are valuable enough to buy passage out of Greyfen forever.

### Old Sem (unaffiliated)

A blind hermit who lives in a turf hut near Harrowgate's western mouth. Sem claims to have crossed the pass seventeen times and says the fog "speaks, if you know how to listen." He is either mad or the most knowledgeable guide to Harrowgate alive. He charges nothing for his guidance but insists on conditions: no torches, no speaking, and absolute trust. Those who follow his rules emerge safely. Those who do not are never seen again. Sem knows more about the moor's history than anyone living and will share it freely, in long, rambling monologues that mix truth with folklore.

### Sister Vrenn (Order of the Ember Quill)

The youngest monk, barely twenty, who joined the Order only two years ago. Vrenn took the vow of silence willingly but has begun to regret it. She communicates through gestures that are increasingly frantic and has been caught twice trying to enter the undercroft alone. The other monks watch her with concern. Vrenn knows something — or thinks she does — about what is sealed below, and her agitation is growing by the day.

## Threads

### The Sealed Undercroft

What did the monks seal beneath the abbey thirty years ago? The Silence killed half the Order and left the survivors mute by choice. Brother Maren hints at "a voice that should not have answered." Warden Thessa will go to any length to keep the undercroft sealed. But the seals are old, the peat fire that supposedly reinforces them must be fed constantly, and Sister Vrenn's obsession with entering suggests the thing below may be calling again. If the fire goes out or the seals break, whatever the monks imprisoned will be free — and the Ashenmoor will learn why the Order chose silence.

### The Peat War

Greyfen's survival depends on peat, and the best bogs lie on land the abbey claims. Reeve Callum's patience is running out. Fen Dysart's discovery of carved tablets near the stones has emboldened the younger cutters, who see the monks' warnings as superstition designed to protect a monopoly. The monks, unable to explain their reasons without breaking silence, can only watch as the cutters push deeper into forbidden ground. If Dysart's tablets reach the wrong hands, scholars and treasure-seekers will flood the moor — and the delicate truce between Order and village will collapse.

### The Fog's Hunger

People disappear in Harrowgate pass. One in ten, the villagers say, but the rate has been climbing. Old Sem insists the fog is not natural — that it is a boundary, not weather, and that something on the other side is "leaning against the door." The monks' solstice vigils at the standing stones may be connected: the lights seen in the circle pulse in rhythm with the fog's thickening. If the disappearances continue, Greyfen will be cut off entirely, unable to trade or flee. And if Sem is right about the fog being a door, then someone — or something — is trying to open it from the other side.
